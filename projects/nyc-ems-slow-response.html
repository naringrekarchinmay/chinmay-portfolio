Predicting Slow NYC EMS Response Risk

A leakage-controlled, time-ordered study on 10.1 million NYC EMS dispatch records. It asks whether what a dispatcher knows the moment a call comes in can predict an unusually slow response, and whether ensemble models do that any better than a single model does.

Harrisburg University · ANLY 530 · Group 8

Emergency medical services work under a simple constraint: a dispatched unit either arrives quickly or it does not, and what decides which happens is already in place before anyone chooses how to respond. If those conditions can be read the moment a call comes in, a dispatcher learns something they would otherwise only find out by watching the clock.

So the study asks whether call-intake information on its own can predict a slow response. Slow here means a response time above the 75th percentile for the call's own severity level, so the bar moves with severity instead of sitting at a single citywide cutoff. It also asks whether ensemble methods do this better than single models. Both questions are settled under time-ordered validation on 10.1 million New York City dispatch records from 2019 to 2025.

The word leakage-aware carries most of the weight. The published records cover the full incident lifecycle, and most of their fields only become known once a unit is dispatched or arrives. Several of them give the outcome away almost directly. A careless model would “predict” response speed from information that exists only because the response already happened. To stop that, every one of the 31 retrieved fields is labeled by the point in the lifecycle when it becomes known, the model is limited to the 13 available at intake, and that boundary is enforced in code and checked with unit tests.

The results are reported with the same care that went into the model. Ensembles do come out ahead on macro F1 and precision, but only by about 0.03. On recall, the metric this project treated as primary, a plain logistic regression beats three of the four ensemble methods, and the stacking model that was chosen clears it by just 4.1 points. Every headline metric looked better on the protected 2025 year, but that was the city's own slow-response rate drifting upward under a fixed threshold, not the model improving. The skill is real but small: PR-AUC 0.5645 against a 0.4014 baseline. And the model is not fit for individual-level decisions, because it never once puts a probability above 0.8585 or below 0.1401.

Highlights

What makes it rigorous.

🔒

A tested leakage boundary

All 31 dispatch fields are sorted by when they become known during an incident. Only the 13 available at call intake are used, and the boundary is enforced in code and covered by unit tests.

⏱️

Time-ordered validation

Expanding yearly folds that always train on the past and validate on the next year. The most recent complete year, 2025, was frozen and opened only once, as a protected test set.

🪜

A seven-model ladder

Seven models, from a majority-class dummy up through logistic regression, a decision tree, random forest, gradient boosting, and soft voting, to a stacking ensemble. All were compared at their default settings.

🎯

A severity-relative target

“Slow” means above the 75th percentile of response time within a call’s own severity level, not a single citywide cutoff. That makes it roughly a one-in-four classification problem.

🧮

Same-rows comparators

Every headline metric is measured against a trivial baseline on the same rows. Three fold-matching mistakes that had quietly inflated earlier claims were caught and fixed.

🗺️

Geography over the clock

Dispatch area and recent call volume matter most; the hour of the day ranks seventh of thirteen. The model reads system load and location, not rush hour.

The edge is small but genuine: PR-AUC 0.5645 against a 0.4014 baseline (a 1.41× lift), and precision about 1.25 times the base rate. Every metric looks better on 2025, but that is the slow-response rate itself climbing from 0.2495 to 0.4014. The prevalence moved under a fixed threshold; the model did not get better. On the 2025 confusion matrix, false positives (431,595) come out almost even with true positives (431,923), so about half of what the model flags is a false alarm. It also never commits: no predicted probability goes above 0.8585 or below 0.1401. That is why it can rank incidents better than chance and still be a poor basis for any single decision.

Approach

How it was built.

01

Retrieve & audit

Query the FDNY EMS Incident Dispatch Data (NYC Open Data, dataset 76xm-jjuj) through the Socrata API for 2019 to 2025, recording a SHA-256 checksum of the pull. The result is 10,881,496 incidents.

02

Construct the population

An ordered set of exclusions removes reopened, cancelled, and transfer incidents, records with no completed response, and physically impossible zero or negative response times. That leaves 10,149,631 incidents, 93.3% of the pull.

03

Define the target

Mark an incident slow when its validated response time exceeds the 75th percentile within its initial severity level, with the threshold computed on training data only and never on the test year.

04

Draw the leakage boundary

Sort all 31 fields by the lifecycle stage when they become known, whether at intake, dispatch, arrival, or as an outcome. Keep only the 13 known at intake, and unit-test the boundary so nothing downstream can leak in.

05

Encode features

Six calendar derivatives, two categorical intake fields (call type and severity), two geographic ones (borough and dispatch area), and three historical aggregates (prior-hour and prior-24-hour counts, plus an expanding mean response by severity). One-hot encoding expands these to 114 columns.

06

Build the ladder

Fit seven models, from a dummy up to a stacking ensemble whose base learners are logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting under a logistic meta-learner trained on out-of-fold predictions. Every model runs at its default settings, so the comparison is between model families rather than tuning budgets.

07

Validate in time order

Score every model on expanding yearly folds that validate on 2021 through 2024, and select on slow-response recall subject to a minimum 0.40 precision floor fixed in advance. Stacking wins, leading the next model by 4.1 points fold for fold.

08

Open the protected year

Score the frozen 2025 configuration on held-out 2025 data once, and write the predictions, base-learner probabilities, and feature matrix to a saved extract that every later analysis reads from.

09

Interpret & stress-test

SHAP and permutation importance as two attribution methods that check each other, a calibration and reliability analysis, and recall and precision across six pre-registered subgroups: borough, dispatch area, severity, call type, hour, and volume.

Interpretation

What the model actually uses.

📍

Geography leads

Dispatch area is the single strongest feature under both attribution methods, and the two geographic fields together outweigh any other pair. Every model improves when borough and dispatch area are included, and two-thirds of the model’s confident false alarms come from one dispatch area.

📈

It reads load, not the clock

Recent call volume ranks above the hour of the day, which sits seventh of thirteen even though the raw rate has a clear daily rhythm. The model has learned that stretches of heavy recent call volume are slow, not that evenings are. That distinction is what matters on an unusually busy morning.

🧩

The ensemble is thinner than it looks

The case for an ensemble rests on its members making different mistakes. Here they largely don’t: the base learners agree on 85.5% of rows, and 40.5% of all errors are unanimous, where no combination rule can help. Stacking buys a modest recall gain, not error diversity.

📐

A comparator must share the rows

The most portable lesson here is methodological: a model score and its baseline only mean something when they are computed on the same rows. Three plausible-looking numbers made it past a first review while comparing across mismatched folds, and each one flipped a headline claim once it was corrected.
